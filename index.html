Featured Research Project — UBC Sensorimotor Physiology Research Internship

Real-Time Visual-Inertial Motion Tracking System

Developing a portable ROS2-based motion-tracking pipeline using a Raspberry Pi camera, IMU sensing, ORB-SLAM3, sensor calibration, and experimental validation.

Supervised by Dr. Jean-Sébastien Blouin — Professor, Sensorimotor Physiology Laboratory, School of Kinesiology, UBC — and Dr. Jesse Charlton — Assistant Professor & Director, Applied Musculoskeletal Biomechanics Laboratory, School of Kinesiology, UBC.

📄 View supervisor reference letter (PDF) ↗

See It In Action

Three clips from testing: the gyro-stabilized pipeline tracking reliably through a full walking loop, the camera-only pipeline running in isolation, and one of the calibration bugs — a gyro under-reading its own rotation — caught on camera mid-test. What the camera and environment were, and how the fix in the first clip came about, follows below.

“I observed demonstrations of their work on several occasions, including a live demonstration of the system operating with the wearable camera while moving about our lab space. Developing such a system involves a number of non-trivial technical challenges, and their work provided them with practical experience applying computer vision and sensor-fusion methods to a real research problem.”

Dr. Jesse Charlton — Assistant Professor & Director, Applied Musculoskeletal Biomechanics Laboratory, School of Kinesiology, UBC

Overview

This work is part of a Sensorimotor Physiology Research Intern position at UBC (Jan–Aug 2026), on real-time sensing, visual-inertial SLAM, and human motion analysis. It grew out of earlier motion-tracking and biomechanics work on the same platform: a Red Pitaya interface streaming real-time IMU data, and a Python pipeline connecting that stream into OpenSim for real-time motion visualization and biomechanical analysis. I also investigated sensor orientation estimation and fusion — working with the VQF algorithm and reading through the xioTechnologies Fusion (imufusion) library's AHRS implementation as reference (third-party code, not something I authored).

That background led toward visual and visual-inertial SLAM. The resulting system streams camera and IMU data from a Raspberry Pi into a ROS2/WSL environment running ORB-SLAM3, producing a live, scale-corrected trajectory that I validated first against the TUM RGB-D benchmark, then against known-distance walking tests on the custom hardware.

Problem / Goal

Build a portable, real-time visual-inertial tracking system from consumer-grade hardware, and prove it's accurate enough for real motion/biomechanics research — not just a demo.

  • Consumer-grade hardware only. A Raspberry Pi camera and a low-cost IMU — no off-the-shelf motion-capture rig.
  • Real-time, not post-processed. The pipeline had to produce a live trajectory, not an offline reconstruction.
  • Prove it, don't assume it. An off-the-shelf SLAM pipeline wasn't guaranteed to work on non-standard hardware — accuracy had to be validated against a known benchmark, not assumed.

My Role

As the research intern on this project, I owned the pipeline end to end:

  • Sensor-streaming foundation. Built the Red Pitaya IMU-streaming interface and the Python/OpenSim pipeline for real-time motion visualization and biomechanical analysis.
  • Visual SLAM pipeline. Integrated ORB-SLAM3 into a live ROS2 pipeline and extended it toward visual-inertial fusion.
  • Camera/IMU bridge. Wrote the streaming and bridge scripts connecting the Raspberry Pi camera and IMU into ROS2.
  • Calibration. Built a custom fisheye calibration workflow around OpenCV.
  • Debugging. Diagnosed and fixed several synchronization and initialization bugs.
  • Validation. Designed and ran the physical experiments that validated tracking accuracy.

ORB-SLAM3, OpenCV, OpenSim, and the Fusion AHRS library are third-party tools I integrated, configured, and in some cases patched — not algorithms I invented.

System / Architecture

Hardware: a TechNexion TEVM-AR0234-C-S191-IR-RPI15 camera module for Raspberry Pi, acquired on a Raspberry Pi and streamed over TCP — the camera was never a local USB webcam, which is why a custom acquisition/calibration pipeline was necessary. Per TechNexion's spec sheet, the module carries an onsemi AR0234 global-shutter sensor (1920×1200, MIPI CSI-2) behind a 191.28°-diagonal fisheye lens (the "S191" in the part number — matching the extreme distortion discussed below), plus an onboard ST LSM6DSO16IS IMU, which I sampled at approximately 208 Hz.

ROS2 was the runtime communication layer, not just "data organization" — it let independently-running nodes (camera bridge, IMU bridge, SLAM node, trajectory publisher, dashboard) publish and subscribe without tight coupling, carried per-message timestamps critical for sync, and exposed runtime parameters (e.g. scale correction) that could be changed without relaunching the pipeline.

WSL/ROS2 + ORB-SLAM3 was chosen over a MATLAB-based pipeline for this stage of the work: MATLAB doesn't run the pipeline in real time, has no built-in fisheye camera model equivalent to ORB-SLAM3's Kannala-Brandt implementation, and has much weaker IMU/visual-inertial fusion support.

Challenges & Debugging

Failures were part of the process, not a footnote — tracking initially failed, calibration needed independent validation, and several distinct bugs had to be isolated one at a time. Here's where it ended up, followed by the problems that had to be worked through to get there.

Where it ended up

After the gyro scale/axis fix and the ChArUco recalibration described below, the pipeline went from dropping tracking within seconds to holding a stable lock through real movement tests.

Getting there took working through several distinct problems, one at a time:

First hardware attempt: not going okay

Before trusting the pipeline on real hardware, I validated ORB-SLAM3 against the EuRoC MH_01_easy dataset. The first run on the actual TEVS camera and Raspberry Pi rig was a different story: the trajectory's start and end height didn't match, and the plotted path showed the camera moving backward for a segment where, on video, it clearly moved forward two steps. That mismatch was the first sign that calibration and axis conventions — not just "SLAM working or not" — needed real scrutiny.

Isolating camera-only and IMU-only before blaming either one

Rather than keep debugging the combined mono-inertial pipeline, I ran the camera and IMU separately. Camera-only tracking (no IMU) was inconsistent between runs — a repeat of the same short path produced two visibly different loops. IMU-only dead-reckoning (no camera/SLAM) showed the orientation drift you'd expect from integrating gyro/accel alone with no visual correction. Neither subsystem was trustworthy in isolation yet, which reframed the problem: get camera-only and IMU-only each behaving predictably first, then combine them.

Why the combined camera+IMU run looked worse than either alone

The first live mono-inertial test tracked briefly then dropped to RECENTLY_LOST. Six candidate explanations were on the table: wrong gyro scale, a timestamp/dropped-sample problem, wrong gyro units (deg/s vs the rad/s ORB-SLAM3 and ROS expect), wrong axis mapping between the IMU and camera frames, a wrong camera-to-IMU transform (Tbc), or camera/IMU samples simply not being synchronized. To test the axis and scale hypotheses directly, I ran a controlled 360° rotation test on the IMU-only dead-reckoning path: rotating the rig exactly 360° integrated out to only about 315° — roughly a 12.5% under-read, giving a correction ratio of 360 / 315 ≈ 1.143, though I treated that as a hypothesis to confirm across repeated tests rather than a number to apply blindly. Axis mapping was checked against the expected rotation-plane-to-gyro-channel relationship (rotation in the X–Z plane should appear on gyro_y, X–Y on gyro_z, Y–Z on gyro_x) before accepting the scale correction.

  • Wrong network path. A stale Windows portproxy entry was forwarding to an old, dead Pi IP (192.168.137.92) instead of the active one (192.168.137.209). Camera frames never reached ROS2, which looked like a SLAM failure but was actually a networking misconfiguration.
  • Calibration needed real data, not guesses. An initial calibration with guessed fx/fy (600.0, 600.0) and zeroed distortion coefficients produced 21% obviously-wrong map points — a 191° fisheye lens has ~75.6% distortion, far too extreme for uncalibrated estimates to work. A first checkerboard-based calibration-capture pass then struggled with that same extreme distortion — many frames were logged as "checkerboard: not found" because the warped board didn't fit a standard corner detector. Switching to a ChArUco board (marker-based, robust to partial visibility) finally gave a clean calibration: 74/74 frames accepted at ~0.39 px reprojection RMS.
  • PredictStateIMU() crash. The mono-inertial pipeline could segfault when inertial prediction ran without enough valid IMU samples for the current image interval. Fixed by gating inertial processing behind a minimum-sample check (≥2 usable IMU samples) and adding NO_IMU_RECEIVED diagnostics tracking sample count, measured frequency, and accel/gyro magnitude.
  • Clock misalignment. The Raspberry Pi's clock was initially off by several days, and after fixing that, a residual ~10–12 second offset still needed investigation — significant when the IMU runs at 208 Hz but images arrive far less often.
  • Stationary-gating bug. A staged test flow waited for a stable period before starting a movement trial, but at 208 Hz a single noise spike could reset an otherwise-valid multi-second stationary countdown. Fixed with a ~150 ms debounce/grace window rather than just loosening the motion threshold.
  • Motion blur limited tracking more than calibration did. A live test (\"Attempt C\") reached 74.8% frames tracked OK, but the effective incoming frame rate was only ~2.9 Hz versus a much higher camera-side target — pointing at streaming/encoding throughput and exposure settings, not intrinsics, as the next bottleneck. Shutter/gain (e.g. 4000/16 → tested against 1000/4) were tuned to trade brightness for reduced blur.
  • Monocular scale was wrong by 9.36×, not 0.94×. A 5 m walking test produced a trajectory reading ~0.534 m; the correction factor is 5 / 0.534 ≈ 9.36. This became a reusable scale-calibration workflow (a scale_calibration_watcher applying a saved factor via ROS2 parameters) instead of a one-off manual multiplication.
  • Physical straight line ≠ SLAM's +X axis. Monocular SLAM has no gravity/compass reference tying its world frame to the room — the world axes are defined by the initial camera pose. A straight physical walk can show up as motion across several SLAM axes; this is a constant rotational offset, not random tracking failure.
  • Hardware failure. The camera fell during a measured-distance test and the FFC ribbon cable connecting it to the Pi disconnected/was damaged — diagnosed as a likely connector-level fault rather than assuming the camera or Pi itself was destroyed.

With the gyro scale correction applied, the axis mapping confirmed, and the ChArUco calibration rolled out, the retest at the top of this section is what came out the other side.

Testing / Validation

Before testing on custom hardware, I validated the Visual SLAM pipeline against the TUM RGB-D benchmark — a standard research dataset with ground-truth camera trajectories — and measured an absolute trajectory error (RMSE) of approximately 2.5 cm. Once the pipeline could track reliably on real hardware, testing shifted from "does SLAM run?" to "does the trajectory correspond to real movement?" — stationary tests (to characterize baseline jitter from image noise, vibration, and IMU bias rather than expecting a mathematical zero), a 5 m known-distance walking test for scale calibration, and directional tests examining individual axis displacement, initialization stability, and trajectory shape. A Vicon motion-capture comparison is the planned next step — run the same physical motion through both systems and compare position, scale, orientation, and drift, rather than assuming simultaneous operation alone would improve ORB-SLAM3.

What I Learned

The biggest lesson was isolating variables in a system with many moving parts: camera-only runs to remove the IMU as a variable, dataset baselines to rule out hardware before blaming the algorithm, and diagnostics (tracking state, feature counts, IMU sample rate) instead of judging the system from a single trajectory screenshot. Several "SLAM problems" — the portproxy misroute, the scale error, the axis-offset confusion — turned out to be systems or math-convention issues once properly isolated, which changed how I approach debugging integrated hardware/software pipelines going forward.

Featured Research Project

FormaLink — BIM & 3D Workspace

A browser workspace for inspecting, measuring, and layering data onto IFC/BIM and 3D models.

▶ Try the live app — formalink-app.netlify.app

Overview

This started as a browser-based CAD editor: a Three.js STL viewer/editor with face selection, scaling, color changes, primitive creation, undo/redo, and AI-driven voice commands for those same operations (plus group actions, importing .glb models, and random model generation). It has since grown into FormaLink, a browser workspace that loads GLTF/GLB/IFC/CAD project files into an interactive 3D "coordination view" with object inspection, measurement, and digital-twin data layers — aimed at making BIM/3D model review possible without desktop software.

Problem / Goal

Desktop BIM/CAD tools are the standard way to inspect a building or 3D model, but they're heavy to set up just to look something up or take a measurement. The goal was a browser tool that could load a model — whether a simple mesh or a full IFC building — and make it explorable: select anything, see its properties, and layer operational data on top.

My Role

I built the original Three.js STL editor and its voice-command layer, then built out FormaLink's model loading (GLTF/GLB/IFC), the Object Inspector, measurement and section-plane tools, boolean operations, the digital-twin data layers, and the in-app AI chat assistant. Three.js, the IFC-parsing stack, and the underlying AI/chat API are third-party tools I integrated and built on top of — not something I wrote from scratch.

System / Architecture

FormaLink loads models (.gltf, .glb, .ifc, .cadproject) into a Three.js viewport with orbit/zoom/select navigation, fixed view angles (Top/Front/Right/Iso/Fit/Back), and switchable backgrounds (Light/Blueprint/Dark/Grid). Selecting any element opens the Object Inspector (dimensions, area, volume, color, transform); a separate toolset adds section planes with XYZ control and boolean operations (Union/Subtract/Intersect). A "Digital Twin" panel overlays simulated Energy, Occupancy, and Maintenance data with live weather and time-based simulation, and level filtering isolates individual floors in multi-story buildings. Projects can be saved/reopened with autosave recovery, exported as GLB, and edited with full undo/redo — all backed by an in-app AI chat assistant for model queries and commands.

Engineering Process

Started with STL loading and display in Three.js, added selection and basic transforms (scale, color), then undo/redo and primitive creation, then a voice-command layer mapped onto those same operations.

Moving to FormaLink meant adding IFC parsing alongside the existing GLB/GLTF path, building the Object Inspector and measurement/section/boolean tools, then layering on the digital-twin data (energy/occupancy/maintenance, weather, time simulation) and the chat assistant last, once the core viewer was solid.

Challenges & Debugging

The main difficulty was keeping AI-driven edits (voice commands in the CAD-editor phase, chat commands in FormaLink) consistent with the same undo/redo history as direct manipulation, so an AI-issued edit could be undone exactly like a mouse-driven one. Supporting IFC alongside mesh formats (GLTF/GLB/STL) in a single object model was the other major piece — IFC carries semantic building metadata that a raw mesh doesn't, so the Object Inspector and selection logic had to handle both without treating IFC as "just another mesh."

Testing / Validation

Validated against a range of sample files spanning both formats: Khronos glTF sample models (Sponza Atrium, Geodesic Dome, Transit Hub, Biome Greenhouse, Industrial Complex, Glass Skyscraper) and IFC building samples (an RME MEP sample project, a heritage-building IFC model), checking that loading, selection, measurement, and the data-layer tools behaved consistently across both.

Results

A deployed, working app — not just a local demo. You can open the live link above, load any of the built-in sample models, and use the inspector/measurement/digital-twin tools directly.

What I Learned

Voice/chat input is far more reliable when it's constrained to a well-defined set of operations than when it tries to accept open-ended natural-language editing — mapping AI commands onto the exact same functions the UI buttons call (rather than a separate code path) is what kept undo/redo trustworthy. I also learned how much a project's scope can grow once you find the right frame for it — a small STL editor became a genuinely useful BIM coordination tool once IFC support and data layers were added.

Project — Structural Optimization Research Assistant, Summer 2026 In Progress

Topology Optimization of a Marine Thruster Foundation

Minimizing structural deformation under a 30% material budget for an underwater tugboat thruster foundation, using a MATLAB SIMP/MMA pipeline adapted from a published benchmark code.

Overview

Topology optimization finds the material layout that minimizes structural deformation (compliance) for a given amount of material — here, at most 30% of the available design space. The starting point was top88mma.m: an MMA-optimizer (Method of Moving Asymptotes) version of the well-known 88-line SIMP topology-optimization code published by Andreassen et al. (2010). That reference code solves a simple cantilever benchmark (a half MBB-beam) under one point load. I modified it into top88_tugboat.m to solve a different, more realistic problem: an underwater structure supporting propulsion thrusters, loaded by two separate thruster forces plus hydrostatic water pressure, fixed to a wall on one side with a sliding support on another.

Problem / Goal

Find a material layout for the thruster foundation that minimizes compliance under real operating loads — two thruster loading conditions plus hydrostatic pressure, at a 30% volume fraction — rather than the single idealized point load the reference benchmark was built for.

My Role

As a Structural Optimization Research Assistant (Summer 2026), I executed and modified the MATLAB topology-optimization code — adapting top88mma.m's problem formulation, mesh, loads, and boundary conditions for the tugboat case — and applied density-based SIMP to investigate efficient material distributions. I also studied alternative approaches (Level-Set, BESO), comparing their computational efficiency, performance, and parameter sensitivity against SIMP. The SIMP method, the MMA optimizer, and the original 88-line benchmark are established techniques from the literature, not something I invented — my contribution was adapting and applying them to this specific engineering problem.

System / Architecture

The tugboat problem uses a 60×30-element mesh (versus the original benchmark's 60×20), a 30% volume fraction, and multiple simultaneous load cases (two thruster forces + hydrostatic pressure) against a fixed-wall-plus-roller support condition — all defined in MATLAB and iterated with the MMA optimizer under SIMP penalization. The results below show that domain converging under different filter-radius and penalization settings.

Engineering Process

Started from top88mma.m, defined the tugboat domain/loads/boundary conditions to produce top88_tugboat.m, then ran a parameter sweep rather than accepting the first converged result: varying the filter radius (rmin) from 0.5 up to 3–5, the penalization exponent, and the volume fraction, to understand how each choice traded off mathematical optimality against manufacturability. Level-Set and BESO were then studied separately as alternative optimization approaches for comparison.

Challenges & Debugging

The main engineering challenge was translating an academic single-load-case optimization script into one that handled multiple simultaneous, physically meaningful load cases for a real marine structure — and then recognizing that the optimizer's mathematically best answer wasn't automatically the right engineering answer. At a small filter radius (rmin = 0.5), the optimizer converged to a lower-compliance design (~1465) with many small holes in a checkerboard-like pattern, very thin members, and sharp angles — lower deformation on paper, but effectively impossible to manufacture (thin walls fail under precision, fatigue, and stress-concentration constraints that the optimizer doesn't model). Increasing rmin to 3.0 forced thicker, smoother members at a modest compliance cost, producing a design that could actually be built.

The same trend showed up at a different problem scale too: a baseline cantilever run at rmin = 1.5 reached ~65 compliance with thin, complex features, while rmin = 4 on the same problem reached ~75 with visibly smoother, thicker geometry. Neither result is "wrong" — they represent different points on the same optimality-vs-manufacturability tradeoff, which is a real engineering decision, not a bug to fix.

Filter radius wasn't the only knob I swept — the SIMP penalization exponent has a similar effect. At rmin = 1.5, going from the standard penal = 3 to a stronger penal = 4 pushed intermediate densities harder toward clean 0/1 solid-or-void decisions, trading a small compliance increase (~65 → ~68) for a visibly simpler, blacker-and-whiter structure — a second, independent way to trade optimality for manufacturability besides the filter radius.

Testing / Validation

Compared convergence and resulting material layout across SIMP, Level-Set, and BESO approaches to sanity-check that the optimized geometry wasn't an artifact of one specific method, and separately ran the rmin/penalization/volume-fraction sweep described above to characterize how sensitive the result was to each optimization parameter rather than reporting a single unvalidated run.

Results

A converged material distribution for the foundation under combined thruster and hydrostatic loading, plus a characterized tradeoff between compliance and manufacturability across filter-radius settings.

What I Learned

Manufacturing constraints aren't restrictions on an otherwise-perfect answer — they're part of the actual engineering problem. A mathematically lower-compliance design with paper-thin, checkerboarded members is useless if it can't be built, inspected, or trusted not to fail; the job isn't to find the mathematically perfect solution, it's to find the best solution that can actually be built, maintained, and operated in the real world. Running the same problem across a range of filter radii, rather than accepting the first converged result, is what made that tradeoff visible instead of implicit.

What I Learned

Server-authoritative design and deterministic/shared simulation state matter far more than they seem to at first — most of the hard bugs were about keeping multiple clients' views of the same game state consistent, not about the game logic itself.
